The idea

Two goals, named up front. Every slide after this one is evidence toward one of them.

Ship a real game that carries a feeling

Not a demo. A finished, public word game with a specific character: cozy, unhurried, built on recognition. A puzzle had to clear an emotional bar, not just a validity check, and I wanted strangers to be able to play it at a real address.

Two distinct learnings, deliberately taken on together: building production software through AI tools as a non-programmer, and designing an agentic pipeline that authors content to a taste it does not hold itself.

The game

Four words, in order.

Connections, but with analogies: a 4×4 board of sixteen words hides four analogy sets. Tap four words to form A : B :: C : D, seed is to tree as spark is to fire, review the frame, press Confirm.

  • The whole board is the theme; each analogy holds a different relationship. The challenge is finding the relationships through sixteen similar-themed words that all seem to belong together.
  • Order is the game. A submission is never sorted. The ordered claim is the claim (a true reading is accepted even when it isn't the stored one). Half the machinery in this deck follows from that one choice.
  • The fourth tap fills the frame without submitting. You get to look at your own claim before you make it.
  • “So close!” (right four words, wrong order) costs a mistake and clears the selection. The penalty is also the hint. Repeating an identical failed guess is free; a different order is a new claim.

If you arrived here from the game, this list is the part you couldn't see. If you haven't played yet, it takes one round to feel why order matters.

The feel

Recognition, not speed.

The puzzle is not “which words are similar?” It is “what relationship repeats across these pairs?”

Four sets, four difficulty tiers. Tiers are revealed on solve and never shown on the live board, so the difficulty is something you discover, not something you're warned about.

GREENYELLOW REDBLACK

Cozy is a constraint, not a mood. No timers, no confetti, no particles. Mistake pips are coffee beans and never red. Lose on the fourth mistake and the loss screen reveals every unsolved set with its explanation, so you always find out what you missed.

I declared the taste bar on day one, not once the pipeline made it necessary: it is not enough for a set to be valid, it has to feel like something. That standard has a name later in this deck, and a machine gets graded against it.

This deck is how ASTO was made. The game design document (v0.13, living) is what it was meant to be. They are companion reads.

The method

Why an agentic pipeline.

A puzzle game is hungry. The design needs a steady supply of boards that all clear the same bar, and I wanted the making of them to be the thing I learned.

One model call can't hold the job. A publishable board has to satisfy sixteen unique words, one set per difficulty tier, four distinct relationship types, no accidental cross-set groupings, fair ordering, and a theme that feels like something. Asked for all of that at once, a model quietly trades constraints away and nobody can say which step failed.

So the job is split into agents with one job each: an author, a grouper, a rater, a builder, a row of evaluators. Each has a declared input and output schema. Each can be tested, measured and improved alone. When a board fails, the failure has an address.

And one judgement is never delegated: whether a board ships. Taste stays human. The machine's job is to make my judgement cheaper to apply, not to replace it.

The intuition going in was that generating good pairs is the difficult part and assembling them is bookkeeping. That is backwards.

from my first pipeline's lessons-learned

I learned that the hard way: my first version was a five-agent Python crew, and it worked. I retired it anyway and rebuilt inside the game's own repo, where the pipeline imports the game's own validators directly. One schema, no drift between the thing that writes the puzzles and the thing that plays them.

The method

Tests are how I read code.

I'm not a programmer by training. I direct the code rather than write it, and that only works if trust never depends on my ability to eyeball a diff.

  • Every session starts and ends the same way. A warmup that re-reads the plan, the log and the open decisions; a wrapup that verifies, records and merges. The repo carries its own memory, so no session depends on mine.
  • Documentation is part of the codebase. A build plan where every decision records what was recommended, what I chose, the risk I accepted, and the trigger for changing my mind. A dev log where every entry says what was verified.
  • Verified states only. Implementation rides work branches; the main branch only ever holds states that passed the full gate, so any point in its history is a safe place to roll back to.
1,440tests passing, zero failing, on the platform's own test runner
0dependencies: no framework, no build step, nothing in package.json

The suite is the mechanism that makes this whole approach viable: it is what turns “it works” from a feeling into a fact I can check without reading the diff. Nothing ships on my confidence.

The method

Nine agents, one deterministic gate, one human.

Every agent is a pure function with a declared input and output schema, and each one has exactly one job. The run is unattended: I'm only involved once a complete candidate board exists. The diagram is the tour; three stops deserve a word.

The revision proposer reads my feedback and drafts the fix brief: it proposes, never authors. Auto-revise is the one bounded repair the machine may make before I see a board, only on findings I explicitly allowlisted, and taste never triggers it.

  • 04a Integrity gate is deterministic code, no model: it checks every rule that can be checked exhaustively, including all 43,680 ordered four-word claims for accidental valid analogies.
  • 06 Adversarial solver tries to break the board the way a sharp player would: cross-set groupings, second readings. Its findings come back in the delegation lesson.
  • 09 Glossary author writes one definition for the board's hardest word, and is allowed to decline: a definition that gives the set away is worse than none.

The method

Judgement needed a room of its own.

The pipeline only makes candidates. What made the game possible is the other half: a local Review Studio where every board meets its editor. I start runs there, play the candidate exactly as a player would, and read each agent's report beside the thing it judged. The feedback form lives there too: all 726 judgements that tune the agents were recorded in that room, and approving a board publishes it through the same validators the game itself loads.

It grew with the project: first review and publish, then the player-ratings panel, then a hand editor where I fix a board in place, every save validated server-side and recorded as before-and-after signal. And it keeps the architecture honest: the Studio reads state only through the core's public queries and changes it only through commands. If it couldn't be built through those seams, that would be the architecture's failure, not the Studio's.

The method

The model has limits. The agents were redesigned around them.

The pipeline's early failures were not prompt problems. They were me learning what a model structurally cannot do. Four principles came out of them, each paid for by a real dead run.

  • Judgement gets slack. A brief that supplies exactly enough pairs makes discarding a weak one fatal, so every brief now leaves room to discard, enforced at every door.
  • Retries step the effort down, not just up. When an agent's thinking overruns its output ceiling, the retry lowers the deliberation budget instead of raising the ceiling. A theme that had died completed on the first down-stepped retry.
  • Every retry must change its input. Three identical attempts against an unchanged pool returned three identical refusals. A retry that changes nothing is resampling.
  • Never ask a model to prove what code can check. One stage was asked to verify no two sets could regroup, combinatorial over sixteen words, while the deterministic gate already proved it exhaustively on every run. That job moved to the gate.

What I learned

Borrowed expertise beats invented expertise.

Every board was starting to feel the same, and the corpus said why: ~80% of all 284 pairs ever authored were the same relationship, one thing becoming or producing another. The obvious next move was to write my own taxonomy of relationship types from taste, and steer the pipeline with it.

I can't be fully relied on to create a foundation for analogies because I am not an expert. I'm merely interested in playing them.

the note where I stopped myself

So I didn't write one. I went and found one: Bejar, Chaffin & Embretson (1991), 10 families and 79 relation types, developed at ETS to classify GRE verbal analogy items, reachable through SemEval-2012 under CC-BY. I compiled it into a controlled vocabulary the pipeline actually runs on: 36 relation types and 8 stances, each with a named failure mode, enforced at four separate doors.

The honest audit

Mapping my existing corpus onto the borrowed taxonomy showed exactly how narrow my own range had been:

  • Family 8, cause & purpose, was roughly 80% of everything.
  • Families 1, 3, 5 and 6 had never appeared at all.

A vocabulary you can count against turns a vague feeling, “these all seem similar”, into a number you can steer with.

Two of the game's earliest locked decisions turned out to be independently validated by that literature: pair order matters, and relation membership is graded rather than binary. The instincts were right. The framework still had to be borrowed.

What I learned

The taxonomy was right, and it didn't work.

Round 1. A board built with one set from each of four formally different families. I played it blind, and it read as “all the same, an object moving forward in time somehow.” I demoted three of the four tiers. The experiment invalidated its own design: formal diversity produced zero felt diversity, because every set still carried an arrow: a direction, a before and an after.

Round 2. Blind again, board letters flipped, with an equally evocative control so the effect couldn't be a theme artefact. The mixed arrowed/arrowless board came back as “the best puzzle yet… This is ASTO.” I rejected the all-arrowed control and named the effect without knowing which board I was holding: “another ‘arrow’ puzzle.”

And then I deliberately did not make it a law

Recorded as decision D-3, marked n=2, hypothesis not law, with a written trigger naming what would have to happen to overturn it. “Let's make sure we don't view the revised principle as immutable.”

The result, as the Studio recorded it

rejected …night-experiment-a all four sets arrowed

Same theme, same night, same evocativeness, letters assigned blind. The only variable was whether every set pointed in a direction.

How taste gets measured at all: the feedback form itself had to be rebuilt around how I actually judge. Its quick tags were compiled from my own prose notes (the four judgements I kept writing because no tag carried them became tags), and board verdicts were separated from set verdicts so rejecting a board never silently rejects a set I praised. It has recorded 726 judgements across 109 boards, and every number in this deck rests on it.

What I learned

Delegation is earned, and bounded.

The machine is allowed to fix a board before I ever see it. It earned that authority against a standard I wrote before the feature existed, and the bound on it is the interesting part.

The case for it was already in my own rejections: I rejected two boards for two specific defects, then found the adversarial solver had flagged both, in almost the same words, with nothing routing its findings anywhere. I was re-deriving by hand what the pipeline had already written down.

[medium] cross-set-association Museum, Louvre… Louvre and Musée d'Orsay are themselves extremely famous museums
[high] unfair spy, alias, operative, cyanide pill near-synonyms… stock spy-gear items
  • The standard came before the feature. Decision D-5, written while there was nothing to grade: about ten fix briefs with recorded verdicts, accepted substantially unedited, before any loop gets proposed.
  • Then the evidence arrived. Fourteen proposal verdicts on the record, every one accepted. On one batch the machine flagged every structural defect I independently caught, before I saw the boards.
The allowlist is the whole authority it has

Chosen explicitly by me and revocable by me: the adversarial solver's cross-set findings at high severity, and the order-ambiguity cluster. Nothing else is read. Taste never triggers a revision, one auto-revision per board, and if the fix still trips the allowlist the board comes to me as it is, carrying the findings and the diagnosis of the failed repair.

Order fairness is the allowlist's other half. A submission is never sorted, so an analogy that reads as truly backwards (seed : bud :: bloom : wilt, where I lost a mistake to a coin flip) is a fairness bug, not a style note. Three detectors now catch that shape: a cross-reading check, a symmetry flag at the deterministic gate, and the test player recording when it had to guess a direction.

All three report and none can block. A wrong fix gets reviewed by a person; a wrong veto silently costs a good board, and nobody ever sees what was lost. Signals, deliberately not gates.

What I learned

An instrument, not a mirror.

A read-only tool re-runs any evaluator against any board in the archive, so the agent is the only variable. Same board, same model, same effort setting, eleven days of prompt work apart.

[Aug 5] the old style guide unity: strong
[Aug 16] the same stage today evocativeness: strong

The second answer is to a question the first version had no field for. The axis did not exist yet, so the old agent could not have been wrong about it. It could only be silent. That axis exists because of a hole my own judgements kept falling through.

Across the whole archive the new axis has returned generic fourteen times, and every one of the fourteen carried a concrete replacement suggestion. The re-run tool itself writes to the terminal and nowhere else: a re-run is commentary, never history.

And it disagreed with me, out loud

I had called that board an absolute snooze. Today's agent answered strong, and went on to praise the exact wording my own note had wanted sharpened. The disagreement was reported as it came back, unedited.

That is the useful result, not the improvement. An evaluator that only ever agrees with you is not an instrument, it is a mirror.

The counterweight: one board, one stage, chosen by the person the machine was flattering. It measures that the agent changed. It does not measure that the agent is right.

What went wrong

Things that were wrong.

The full list in the project log runs to thirteen items and grows with the project on purpose: keeping it is a method, not a confession. These are the ones that taught me the most.

  • The experiment that invalidated its own design. The board I built to demonstrate diversity was the thing that proved my definition of diversity was wrong.
  • The synthetic-feedback near-miss. Four verification runs, carrying feedback an AI had typed to exercise the form, were swept into version control by a git add -A. Synthetic judgement is indistinguishable from real judgement once it is in the file the editorial rubric compiles from. Caught at wrap-up; all four removed.
  • The recurring scar, named as this project's signature failure: a rule enforced at one door and not the one downstream. A pair floor added to the form and not the CLI. A quota checked at the wrong granularity. Evaluator findings produced and never routed. Each one individually reasonable; together, a pattern worth having a name for.
  • The teaching example that shipped. Stage 01 was reproducing its own prompt's example, and a board built that way went live before anyone noticed. A test now proves no generative prompt quotes a complete set.
  • The uncountable corpus. One free-text field made 40% of the pair corpus impossible to count, so the diversity steering ran blind on nearly half its data. I only found it because I went to measure something else.

Forward

Shipped, and still unfinished.

The open loop, named

The editorial rubric does not exist yet. The entire feedback loop was built to compile one; the plan of record said thirty judged boards, and there are 109. It is the oldest unpaid debt in the project, and I would rather name it here than have this deck imply it is done.

Where this goes next

Relics of the Nine, my systems-driven roguelike, is the next project this workflow gets pointed at. The pattern that transfers is the machine's shape: generator, validators, an adversarial check, and a human editor whose taste is the standard everything is measured against.

What transfers

Not the game. The habit of writing down what was decided and why, of naming the trigger that would change my mind before I need it, of granting a machine authority only against evidence I wrote the standard for in advance, of keeping the failed experiment instead of the tidy summary, and of knowing which judgements are mine to make and which ones to go and borrow from someone who actually knows.

Validity can be delegated and checked. Taste is the product.
